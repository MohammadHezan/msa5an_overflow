# Campus Connect can pool rides legally, cautiously

No confirmed 2025–2026 Jordanian rule bans a licensed app from putting several unrelated requests in one car. The governing **Regulation on Passenger Transport Using Smart Applications (reported as No. 74 of 2025, effective 16 October 2025)** is about licensing, LTRC-approved fares, data links and blocking unlicensed apps. The only "ban" in the November 2026 coverage targets trips organised through WhatsApp groups and social-media pages. That makes **Jeeny, an LTRC-licensed operator, the right host**: Campus Connect inherits Jeeny's licence. The real constraint is price. App fares are pegged to the yellow-taxi meter (**46.8 piasters flag fall + 33.6 piasters/km + 5.76 piasters/waiting-minute by day since 11 May 2026**), and any per-seat pooled fare would very likely need LTRC approval. One booker splitting a single approved fare with friends is the lowest-risk path. On the algorithm side, real-time insertion is a sound front end because the single campus origin makes every feasibility check tiny (at most 4! = 24 drop-off orders). Pair it with a set-partitioning re-optimisation over all still-open groups shortly before each group locks. That combination recovers most of what a batch optimiser would find without making riders wait for a batch. For fares, use the prioritised Shapley value (airport rule), capped so nobody pays more than their solo fare, and show riders a guaranteed discounted fare up front.

## No Jordanian text bans pooling, but fares must be LTRC-approved

The brief's premise, a "November 2026 shared-trip ban", does not hold up. The research found no Petra or Ammon report, bylaw article or LTRC decision prohibiting pooled multi-request trips. Law-firm summaries of the 2025 regulation list its key articles: a **JOD 100,000 bank guarantee per company** (Art. 4), a ban on using rider databases for any purpose other than app-based passenger transport (Art. 5(b)), a data-sharing link with the LTRC (Art. 6), a driver age cap of 65 (Art. 10), and LTRC power to have unlicensed apps blocked (Art. 18). None of them addresses shared trips ([Nsair & Partners](https://nsairs.com/2025/11/13/%D8%AA%D9%86%D8%B8%D9%8A%D9%85-%D9%86%D9%82%D9%84-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%B1%D9%83%D8%A7%D8%A8-%D9%85%D9%86-%D8%AE%D9%84%D8%A7%D9%84-%D8%A7%D8%B3%D8%AA%D8%AE%D8%AF%D8%A7%D9%85-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%AA%D8%B7%D8%A8-2/); [Jaradat Law](https://jaradatlaw.com/ar/legislative-update-regulation-of-ride-hailing-and-smart-transport-services-in-jordan-2025/)). The effective date of **16 October 2025** and the core rule that nobody may carry passengers through an app without a licence and a permit are confirmed by [Ammon News](https://www.ammonnews.net/article/1046001). In late November 2026, the LTRC official responsible for smart apps counted **8 licensed apps against 12–13 unlicensed ones** and called trip organising through WhatsApp or social-media pages a violation ([Jo24](https://jo24.net/article/583646)). The likely source of the "pooling ban" story is this crackdown on informal group-chat carpools. Licensed apps matching several riders are a different matter.

Two caveats matter. First, the Official Gazette text itself could not be retrieved. Even the regulation's number is disputed (74 vs. 47 of 2025, per a [JFRA News](https://jfranews.com.jo/article/521031) snippet), and the definitions article might still contain language on "more than one passenger or request". Second, licensees must submit a "clear basis and calculation method" for fares for LTRC approval, and the 2025 regime keeps fare and commission approval ([JordanianLaw.com, 2018 text](https://jordanianlaw.com/%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A3%D9%86%D8%B8%D9%85%D8%A9/%D9%86%D8%B8%D8%A7%D9%85-%D8%AA%D9%86%D8%B8%D9%8A%D9%85-%D9%86%D9%82%D9%84-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%B1%D9%83%D8%A7%D8%A8-%D9%85%D9%86-%D8%AE%D9%84%D8%A7%D9%84-%D8%A7%D8%B3%D8%AA%D8%AE%D8%AF%D8%A7%D9%85-%D8%A7/); [Jaradat Law](https://jaradatlaw.com/ar/legislative-update-regulation-of-ride-hailing-and-smart-transport-services-in-jordan-2025/)). So pooling is not banned, but a new per-seat pooled tariff is a new fare structure, and that almost certainly needs LTRC sign-off. This is an inference, not a confirmed rule.

The single-booker model raises no flags. No source prohibits one student booking one private ride for friends, or the group splitting the cost. In-app splitting of a single approved fare leaves the trip and the fare unchanged. The risk only appears when each rider is charged a seat price that differs from the approved per-trip fare. The sources disagree on the 20% rule. Fact Jo frames app fares as taxi meter **plus at least 20%**, a floor ([Fact Jo](https://factjo.com/news/139861)). Other outlets describe it as a cap of the meter plus at most 20% ([Garaa News](https://garaanews.com/article/406568); [Jo24](https://jo24.net/article/578751)). Either way, a discounted per-seat fare that adds up to less than the floor could look like a violation, and the penalty is steep: **JOD 50,000 of the guarantee is forfeited per violation** ([Garaa News](https://garaanews.com/article/406568)). The safe design is to price the car trip at or above the regulated fare and split that total among riders. There is also a precedent: Jeeny became Al-Ahliyya Amman University's official transport partner in July 2025, offering event-based promo codes but no group or shuttle product ([Jordan News](https://www.jordannews.jo/Section-33/Trade-Industry/Jeeny-Becomes-Official-Transport-Partner-for-AAU-to-Power-Affordable-Student-Mobility-43445)). Campus Connect fits that template: a licensed operator, a university partnership, and pooling layered on top.

Peer-to-peer paid carpooling outside a licensed app is the clear danger. The regulated activity is carrying passengers "for a fee" through apps, and Law No. 19 of 2017 prohibits any passenger transport service without LTRC licensing ([Al Tamimi & Co.](https://www.tamimi.com/law-update-articles/ride-hailing-apps-in-jordan/)). No source addresses university shuttles or zero-profit cost sharing. A women-only filter is not barred by anything found, since driver eligibility rules are gender-neutral ([JordanianLaw.com](https://jordanianlaw.com/%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A3%D9%86%D8%B8%D9%85%D8%A9/%D9%86%D8%B8%D8%A7%D9%85-%D8%AA%D9%86%D8%B8%D9%8A%D9%85-%D9%86%D9%82%D9%84-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%B1%D9%83%D8%A7%D8%A8-%D9%85%D9%86-%D8%AE%D9%84%D8%A7%D9%84-%D8%A7%D8%B3%D8%AA%D8%AE%D8%AF%D8%A7%D9%85-%D8%A7/)), and Amman has had women-driven "pink taxis" serving university students before ([Gulf News/AFP](https://gulfnews.com/amp/story/world%2Fmena%2Fwomen-only-taxi-service-challenges-norms-in-jordan-1.1953992)).

## The 11 May 2026 tariff gives the simulator exact coefficients

Since **11 May 2026**, the yellow-taxi meter everywhere except Aqaba charges **39 piasters flag fall + 28 piasters/km by day** and **40 + 33 at night**, up from 37/38 and 26/32 ([Al-Balad News](https://albaladnews.net/article/461466); [Altaj News](https://www.altaj.news/article/562281)). The LTRC's smart-app schedule, published the same day and binding on Careem, Uber, Petra Ride, Jeeny and Taxi F within a week, is exactly 1.2 times the meter. By day it is **46.8 piasters opening + 33.6/km + 5.76/waiting-minute**. At night it is **48 + 39.6/km + 6.79/minute** ([Khaberni](https://www.khaberni.com/news/808843-here-is-the-new-tariff-schedule-for-smart-application-services-in-jordan---details); [Fact Jo](https://factjo.com/news/139861)). The 1.2 ratio holds to the decimal: 39 × 1.2 = 46.8 and 28 × 1.2 = 33.6. Dividing the app waiting rates by 1.2 implies about 4.8 piasters/min (day) and 5.66 piasters/min (night) for taxis. That is an inference, because no 2026 taxi waiting rate was published. The regulator defines only a *waiting* minute, not a per-minute charge on moving time. A simulator should therefore apply the minute rate only to stop-and-go time, not to the whole trip duration.

The pitch deck's "12 km ≈ JOD 3.75" is exactly right for a daytime yellow taxi with no waiting. A Jeeny ride of the same length costs at least **JOD 4.50**, which is the figure a Campus Connect split should start from. Three parameters are unconfirmed and should stay configurable: the night window (commonly 22:00–06:00, unsourced), the minimum fare (the last official figure is a **JD 1 minimum from 2019**, per a [Jordan Times](https://jordantimes.com/node/291605) snippet), and the real-world markup above the floor (model it as 1.0–1.3×, since no Jeeny, Careem or Uber Jordan rate card was found). The bus baseline of **JOD 0.35–0.65** matches the BRT launch fares (Sweileh–Sports City 0.35, Sweileh–Museum 0.65) but is dated to around 2021 ([Jordan News](https://www.jordannews.jo/Section-109/News/Normal-BRT-fares-go-into-effect-5735)). It is the price pooled rides compete against: four riders sharing a 12 km app trip pay about JOD 1.13 each, roughly twice the bus and a quarter of a solo ride. Campus-to-neighbourhood distances in the notes (for example UJ–Sweileh ≈ 4 km, UJ–Tabarbour ≈ 9 km, AAU–Marka ≈ 35 km) are haversine × 1.35 estimates and should be replaced with OSRM or a distance-matrix API before any real pricing claims.

## Insert in real time, then re-partition before each group locks

Batch optimisation is the textbook approach. Alonso-Mora et al. enumerate every feasible trip, growing it one request at a time and testing a trip of size k only if every sub-trip with one request removed is already feasible. They then solve an ILP that assigns each request exactly once (or leaves it unassigned at a large penalty). At NYC scale, **3,000 four-seat vehicles served 98% of demand with a 2.8-minute mean wait**, on 30-second batches that each solved in under 30 seconds ([PNAS/PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5255617); [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017PNAS..114..462A/abstract)). Campus Connect's choice of real-time insertion is compatible with this. It is what you get from running that pipeline incrementally, and the notes support three design elements for it.

**The insertion step is cheap and exact for one origin.** When a new request arrives, test it against each open group. Insertion is feasible if some drop-off order of the enlarged group (at most 24 orders for four riders) satisfies seat capacity, each member's detour limit, and the departure-window overlap. Alonso-Mora's thresholds give sensible defaults: a maximum wait Ω of 2–7 minutes and a maximum added delay Δ = 2Ω. Santi et al. used a maximum delay of about 5 minutes ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5255617); [arXiv 1310.2963](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1310.2963)). The ratio form, a maximum detour factor γ on pooled versus direct distance, is a common alternative, and a higher γ raises the share rate ([arXiv 2508.19192](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.19192)). In practice, cap each rider's ride at min(γ · solo, solo + X minutes), for example γ = 1.5 and X = 10. Departure windows check in one line: the group is feasible only if the latest earliest-departure falls before the earliest latest-departure. Pick the open group with the largest marginal saving. With a shared depot that is the Clarke-Wright idea, s(i, j) = d(O, i) + d(O, j) − d(i, j), generalised to "solo cost of the new rider minus the added route cost" ([MIT Urban OR book](https://web.mit.edu/urban_or_book/www/book/chapter6/6.4.12.html)). If no group qualifies, open a new singleton group. Gender preference is one more feasibility predicate: a rider who opted into women-only can only join all-women groups. This mirrors how Uber Women Preferences and DiDi Mujer work as hard filters, and both warn of longer waits ([Uber Newsroom](https://www.uber.com/us/en/newsroom/women-preferences/); [Jakarta Post/Reuters](https://www.thejakartapost.com/life/2020/11/25/didi-launches-service-in-mexico-for-women-to-select-only-female-passengers)).

**Greedy insertion is a warm start, not the answer.** Insertion is greedy and order-dependent. Three riders who would form a perfect trio can end up split because the first two were placed before the third arrived. Clarke-Wright-style heuristics carry "no near-optimality guarantee" and can go badly in pathological cases ([MIT Urban OR book](https://web.mit.edu/urban_or_book/www/book/chapter6/6.4.12.html)). Alonso-Mora handles this by using a greedy assignment (largest trips first, cheapest first) only to seed the ILP, which then improves toward the optimum ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5255617)). Industry did the same. UberPOOL reportedly kept re-optimising after a match and sometimes held a rider back when a better match was expected soon, though that source is old and undated ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=1426531)). Uber's current marketplace still batches requests for a few seconds rather than taking the nearest pair ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/gb/en/marketplace/matching/)).

**Re-optimise at lock time with set partitioning.** Give each open group a lock time, for example a few minutes before its departure or when it fills. Just before a lock, gather every rider in groups that are still open. Enumerate their feasible pairs, triples and quads with the sub-trip pruning rule, and solve a set-partitioning model with OR-Tools CP-SAT: one Boolean per candidate group (all singletons included, so the model is always feasible), `AddExactlyOne` per rider, and integer-scaled costs such as metres or fils ([CP-SAT docs](https://developers.google.com/optimization/cp/cp_solver); [OR-Tools allowed groups](https://developers.google.com/optimization/assignment/assignment_groups)). Then commit only the groups that are due to lock and let the rest keep accepting insertions. The problem is small. Even with no pruning, 50 riders produce **1,225 pairs, 19,600 triples and 230,300 quads**, each with at most 24 route orders, and detour limits prune most of them. No published benchmark exists at n ≈ 50, so set `max_time_in_seconds` as a safety net; CP-SAT returns its best incumbent when time runs out. If the stated reason for choosing real-time matching is to avoid making riders wait, this hybrid keeps that benefit: riders see a tentative group immediately, and only the confirmation waits for the lock. For a v0, pairs alone can be solved optimally with maximum-weight matching on savings, as Santi et al. showed for k = 2 ([arXiv 1310.2963](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1310.2963)). Two rules keep the product honest. Never re-assign a rider out of a locked group. And show each rider a fare that can only go down when re-optimisation changes their group.

## Prioritised Shapley splits fairly and computes in microseconds

The fare split has an unusually clean answer for a shared origin. With a fixed drop-off order, the ride-sharing cost game is a 2D generalisation of the **airport problem**. In the collinear case its Shapley value is the Littlechild-Owen rule: split each leg of the route equally among the riders still on board ([Wikipedia: Airport problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airport_problem); [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v20y1973i3p370-372.html)). Levinger, Hazon and Azaria derive a polynomial closed form for this "prioritised" game. They show that exact Shapley becomes NP-hard once the route order is free, and that their SHAPO proxy, which uses the shortest-path order as the priority, came within **4.60% mean error (max US$0.43 on about US$5 fares)** of exact Shapley on Toulouse airport-origin data. "Depot distance" proportional splitting was off by 31.26% ([ar5iv 1909.04713](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1909.04713)). With at most four riders there are only 16 subsets per group, so a hackathon can brute-force the exact prioritised Shapley value using the chosen drop-off order.

Shapley is not automatically individually rational once detours exist. Worked examples (our own arithmetic with the cited formulas) show where each rule breaks. In example A, riders drop off on one road at 4 km and 7 km. Per-leg, Shapley and equal-savings all give **2 and 5 km-equivalents**, while proportional gives 2.55 and 4.45. In example B, the second rider sits 6 km out but off-route, so the pooled route is 8 km. Per-leg charges that rider the full solo 6 km and they **save nothing**, while Shapley gives 3 and 5 and both riders save 1. Equal-savings splitting can push a short-trip rider to zero with three or more riders. Proportional-to-solo is always individually rational when the pooled cost is below the sum of solo costs, but it ignores who caused the detour. The core and nucleolus are theoretically stronger, because they guarantee no sub-coalition prefers to defect, but they need an LP over coalitions ([Lu & Quadrifoglio, arXiv 1902.07266](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.07266)). That is overkill for a demo. The robust combination is to compute the prioritised Shapley value and cap each rider at their solo fare times (1 − minimum discount). Then redistribute any excess pro rata, and enforce a floor so the group total never drops below the LTRC-regulated car fare.

What riders see should follow industry practice. Uber quotes a guaranteed upfront pooled price that already factors in the likelihood of a match, so the shared fare is always below UberX ([Uber POOL FAQ](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/uberpoolfaqs)). Lyft tied the discount to route overlap ([Lyft Eng archive](https://eng.lyft.com/archive)). Advertised savings ranged from about 20% (UberX Share) to 40% (older POOL marketing) ([Uber AU](https://www.uber.com/au/en/ride/uberpool/); [Ridester](https://www.ridester.com/?p=618768)). For Campus Connect, quote each rider an upfront "up to X% off solo" price. Use the capped Shapley split to settle the actual bill, so the final figure is never above the quote.

## Conclusion

The legal and algorithmic questions turn out to share one answer: the car trip, not the seat, is the unit that must be priced, licensed and optimised. Jordan regulates who may carry passengers and what a trip may cost, and it enforces that with a JOD 50,000 penalty per violation. So Campus Connect is safest when each pooled car carries one LTRC-compliant Jeeny fare, and the algorithm decides only who shares it and how it is divided. That framing also tells the team what to verify first. The Gazette text of the 2025 regulation, and Jeeny's view on whether an in-app split of one fare needs new LTRC approval, matter more than any solver tuning.

On the engineering side, the single campus origin collapses the hard parts of ride pooling. Routing a group means checking 24 orders, and fair splitting means checking 16 subsets. The MVP's real design decisions are therefore product parameters: the detour cap γ, the lock time, and the minimum guaranteed discount. These are worth exposing as sliders in the simulator. Judges will see that real-time insertion with lock-time re-partitioning trades a few minutes of tentative grouping for close-to-batch efficiency. The capped Shapley split shows each rider why their share is fair, and it never undercuts the regulated fare for the car.
