# Campus Connect's matcher is sound, but needs fixes

Campus Connect's matching design is well founded. Enumerating every feasible car of up to four riders and solving an exact set partition with CP-SAT is the single-origin special case of the batch method that MIT researchers showed can serve about 98% of Manhattan taxi demand. It is also the structure Uber and Lyft moved toward when they dropped first-come greedy matching. The evidence also says the optimizer is worth having. A head-to-head study found exact batch assignment saves **about 20% vehicle-km over an insertion heuristic at peak**. Running the team's own engine (`pooling/`) on the demo corridors gives a similar result: **CP-SAT used 18–25% fewer car-km than greedy insertion once 16 or more students leave one gate in the same wave**. The gap drops to about 7% (median 0%) with 8 students. Two problems would undercut a live demo. First, the second solver stage (fewest cars at the optimal car-km) **times out at about 28 riders, and the app then sends every student home alone**. Second, in the demo's corridor geography the detour cap and the 20% saving floor never bind, so judges cannot see them work. On thresholds, the 4-seat cap and the women-only filter are well supported. The 1.5× detour cap is at the upper edge of tolerance. The 20-minute class-end gap is the weakest rule: commercial pooling asked riders to wait only 1–2 extra minutes. The recommended objective is "fewest students left alone, then fewest money-weighted car-km", implemented as one weighted objective. In simulation it removes nearly all solo riders for 0.5–4% more car-km, and it also fixes the timeout.

## Campus Connect is Alonso-Mora's batch method shrunk to one gate

The algorithm has a strong pedigree. Alonso-Mora et al. enumerate feasible trips as cliques in a shareability graph. They grow a trip one request at a time, test a k-rider trip only if every (k−1)-rider sub-trip is feasible, and then solve an ILP that assigns each request exactly once under caps on wait, delay (Δ = 2Ω) and capacity, on 30-second batches. With **3,000 four-seat vehicles they served 98% of NYC taxi demand at a 2.7-minute mean wait and 2.3-minute mean delay** ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5255617)). Campus Connect's `regroup.py` uses the same enumeration and the same pruning rule, and it puts an `AddExactlyOne` per rider on a Boolean per candidate car. Because every rider starts at one gate, the pickup-ordering explosion that pushed Lyft into heuristics disappears. Lyft counted **1,776 stop orderings for four passengers versus 4 for two** ([rrojin summary of Lyft Engineering](https://rrojin.tistory.com/21)). Campus Connect only has to check at most 4! = 24 drop-off orders per car.

Production systems arrived at the same structure after starting greedy. Lyft Line's first matcher was greedy on haversine distance. Lyft later moved to "less greedy" global matching framed as weighted maximum matching, widening its window from 60 seconds to a few minutes ([rrojin summary](https://rrojin.tistory.com/21)). Uber's marketplace waits a few seconds to batch requests and optimizes for the whole batch instead of the nearest pair ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/us/en/marketplace/matching/)). Uber's Pool window grew from 90 seconds to up to 5 minutes ([Uber AU](https://www.uber.com/au/en/ride/uberpool/)). Two differences are worth stating openly to judges. Production systems optimize mainly for rider time (detour and pickup), while Campus Connect optimizes car-km and caps rider time with constraints. UberX Share now allows **only two parties per car** with up to 20% off ([Mass Transit](https://www.masstransitmag.com/alt-mobility/shared-mobility/article/21272370/uber-brings-back-shared-rides-as-uberx-share)), while Campus Connect allows four, which the shared origin makes defensible.

The closest production analogue is not city-wide pooling, which mostly failed. Lyft ended Shared rides in 2023 because they "took people out of their way" ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2023/05/11/lyft-carpool-ride-eliminated/)). In 2025 it **revived pooling at 8 US airports**, which are single-origin sites with clustered demand ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/05/19/lyft-pooled-rides-at-airports-cheaper-trips/)). A campus gate after a class block is the same shape of problem. Via's "virtual stops" pursue the same goal in another way, by moving pickups onto the vehicle's path so they add no detour ([US 10,677,604](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10677604)). A single fixed gate is the extreme version of that idea. Campus Connect therefore sits between a campus ride board like Zimride (130+ campuses at its peak, [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=845475)) and a dispatched shuttle. The verified student community also addresses the trust failure that suspended DiDi Hitch in 2018 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/05/15/chinas-didi-pares-back-hitchhiking-car-service-following-passenger-murder)).

## Two solver defects can erase the optimizer's advantage

The design is sound, but the implementation has two defects. A third issue makes the demo hide its own rules. All three were found by running the repository's engine, so they are this report's own analysis, not findings from a published source.

**The second objective stage times out and falls back to "everyone solo".** `regroup()` first minimizes car-km. It then adds the constraint `car_km == optimum` and minimizes the number of cars, with a 5-second, single-worker limit per stage. If either stage returns no solution, the function returns `current`. On the app's matching path (`sharing.group_riders` → `regroup(singles)`), `current` is every rider in their own car. The equality on a weighted sum is a hard feasibility problem for CP-SAT. In tests on the West corridor, the first stage solved to optimality in under 3.3 s for 28 riders (1,890–3,218 candidate cars). **The second stage returned UNKNOWN in all four 28-rider instances tested.** In a 30-seed run, the shipped `regroup` produced 7% car-km savings versus solo at 28 riders, against 62% for greedy insertion, because 27 of 30 runs collapsed to all-solo. The fix is one change: replace the two stages with a single weighted objective. The natural weight is the regulated fare itself. Each car costs 468 fils plus 336 fils/km, so minimizing **336 × car-km + 468 × cars** minimizes the total that students pay. That single objective solved to optimality in about 1 s at 28 riders and gave the same car-km as stage one. A solution hint or a `<=` bound would also work, but the weighted form is easier to explain.

**The sub-group pruning rule is only valid for monotone constraints, and the 20% saving floor is not monotone.** The window, seat, women-only and detour rules are hereditary: if a car of four passes them, every subset passes too, given a triangle-inequality distance. The saving rule is different. A pair can fail it while a trio containing that pair passes, because a third fare-payer lowers everyone's share. A synthetic case shows this. Rider A is 5 km out, riders B and C are 10 km out, and A–B is 8 km. The pair {A, B} fails the 20% floor, so the trio {A, B, C} is never generated. The trio is feasible: a 13 km route where A pays 0.97 JOD against 2.15 solo and B pays 1.94 against 3.83. `regroup` instead returned {B, C} plus A alone, which uses **15 km and one more car**. This case did not occur in the demo corridors or in the 13 real Amman stops checked for AHU (0 of 564 feasible 3–4 rider groups were wrongly pruned). But raising the saving floor, or using real road distances, would expose it. The fix is to prune only on hereditary rules and check savings and quotes afterwards.

**In the corridor demo, the detour cap and the saving floor never bind.** `geo.road_km` measures same-corridor distance as |km difference| and cross-corridor distance as the sum via campus. Dropping riders in km order therefore gives everyone an in-car distance equal to their solo distance, so the detour cap cannot bind. For West and South, which share the Main gate, every one of the 15 cross-corridor pairs fails the cap. Every one of the 194 same-corridor groups of 2–4 riders passes every rule, and the lowest saving among them is **23.1% (Al Jubeiha + Abu Nseir)**. In this geography the only constraints doing work are the class-end window, the women-only filter and the four seats. That is honest to show, but if the pitch claims "detour-protected matching", the demo needs side branches off each corridor or real OSRM distances so judges can watch the cap reject a car.

## Riders pay for minutes, not strangers

The rider-tolerance literature points one way: **added time is the main barrier to pooling**, more than sharing with a stranger. In a Dallas–Fort Worth choice experiment (n ≈ 1,600), avoiding one extra passenger was worth only **$0.49 on a commute, equivalent to 1.1 minutes**. The authors call that "low when compared to actual delays caused by an additional passenger" ([Lavieri & Bhat 2019](https://www.caee.utexas.edu/prof/bhat/ABSTRACTS/WTS_paper.pdf)). An Austin RP/SP study found that **cutting the pooled time penalty by 5 minutes added about 4 pooled riders per 100 commute trips, twice the effect of an extra $1 discount**. An additional co-rider cost only about 1 per 100 ([Kang et al.](https://www.caee.utexas.edu/prof/bhat/ABSTRACTS/PooledRH.pdf)). Dutch respondents valued waiting at 1–1.5× in-vehicle time, and **the per-minute disutility rose as waits grew longer** ([Alonso-González et al. 2020](https://pure.tudelft.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/72155146/1_s2.0_S0968090X1931589X_main.pdf)). Each threshold reads differently against that evidence.

**Detour, min(1.5 × solo, solo + 4 km):** defensible as a ceiling, not a target. Stated-preference designs put pooled options at +25% to +75% time ([Kang et al.](https://www.caee.utexas.edu/prof/bhat/ABSTRACTS/PooledRH.pdf)). A 2026 survey in Shenzhen, Chengdu and Hong Kong treated a 40% detour as "moderate", yet the authors' fitted curve implies only about **12% acceptance at a 20% discount and about 26% at 40%** at that detour ([arXiv 2608.22440](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.22440)). The +4 km term is a useful tightener: it binds for every trip over 8 km. Riders experience minutes, though, and Amman traffic can make 4 km much longer than 4 km's worth of time. A time cap matches the evidence better.

**Wait, class ends within 20 minutes:** the weakest threshold. Uber Express Pool asked riders to accept waits "up to two minutes" longer, and Lyft Shared Saver drivers waited one minute ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2017/11/10/uber-express-pool/amp/); [Autoblog](https://www.autoblog.com/2019/03/02/lyft-shared-saver-uber-express-pool-ridesharing/)). In the team's engine, the earliest student in a car waits up to the full 20 minutes at the gate, and **every simulated scenario hit that 20-minute maximum**. Mean waits were 6–8 minutes. A campus differs from a curb, because students know the pickup time and can use the gap. No study measures tolerance for scheduled pooled pickups, so this is a real evidence gap and should be presented that way. Tightening the window costs efficiency. With a 10-minute gap, mean wait halved (7.3 → 3.6 minutes at 16 riders), car-km rose 16–22%, and solo riders rose from 0.6 to 1.1 per batch. Students still paid an average of **1.74 JOD against 4.18 JOD solo**.

**Saving floor, 20%:** matches the market floor, not a comfortable target. In Chicago, UberPool's implied discount fell from about **49% to 23%** between 2018 and 2019 ([Schwieterman, DePaul](https://trforum.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/The-Changing-Price-of-Ridesharing-in-Chicago.pdf)). Over the same period, pooled trips fell from **about 20% to 9%** of trips ([arXiv 2008.06050](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.06050.pdf)). UberX Share today promises up to 20% off ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2022/06/21/uber-shared-rides-return/)). Campus Connect's actual savings are far higher: across simulated optimal plans the minimum rider saving was 26–50%, and typical shares are 50–75% below solo. The floor is a safety net, so it can rise to 25% at almost no cost, once the pruning defect is fixed.

**Four per car and women-only:** both strongly supported. Both Bhat-group experiments capped co-riders at three for a sedan, and class-to-home trips are commute-like, the purpose with the smallest co-rider penalty ([Lavieri & Bhat](https://www.caee.utexas.edu/prof/bhat/ABSTRACTS/WTS_paper.pdf)). For Jordan specifically, a 630-response study found **women less interested in carpooling because of privacy and trust** ([catalog record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi...........2cee26c08bfa9c1a59443f8f79b36efe)). Women make up 51.8% of Jordanian university students ([FES](https://library.fes.de/pdf-files/bueros/amman/15221.pdf)). The IFC found **44% of women riders would use ride-hailing more with a woman-driver option**, but warns that gender-segregated pools lengthen waits ([IFC](https://www.ifc.org/content/dam/ifc/doc/mgrt/executive-summary-ifc-gender-segregated-transportation-in-ride-hailing.pdf)). The engine shows that trade-off: with 30% of women opting in (about 16% of riders), car-km rose 7–18%, and women-only riders made up about half of the students left alone. The women-only filter is likely an adoption requirement rather than an optional feature, and its cost should be shown rather than hidden.

## A 20–25% gain over greedy is credible, but only in peak waves

The cleanest published comparison re-implemented Alonso-Mora's method for Prague (capacity 5, 4-minute max delay, 30-second batches). At peak, exact assignment drove **429,172 vehicle-km against 539,793 for an insertion heuristic, about 20% less**. It used about 12% fewer vehicles and about 5% less passenger delay. Off-peak, the km gain was about 17%, but the **fleet advantage reversed to about 2% worse**. A time-limited near-optimal solve kept a 16% km gain ([arXiv 2305.02209](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.02209)). Santi et al. give the mechanism: with a 1-minute delay tolerance, 94.5% of NYC trips were shareable with full foreknowledge but under 30% when matched online ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4169909)). Seeing the whole wave before committing is what an optimizer exploits. The literature has no greedy-vs-optimal benchmark for a one-origin campus or airport problem. A 2014 Dulles airport shuttle study reported only that heuristics "provide good approximations" ([TRR](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3141/2467-17)). Campus Connect needs its own number.

That number was computed here with the repository's own `evaluate` and `insert` functions on the West corridor (five stops, 5–16 km). Class ends fell on a 5-minute grid between 15:00 and 16:00, 52% of riders were women and 30% of women chose women-only. Results cover 30 seeds, with greedy insertion run in a random booking order and CP-SAT run with the fixed single objective:

| Riders per wave | Greedy car-km | Optimal car-km | Gap, mean (median) | Solo riders, greedy → optimal | Car-km saved vs. all-solo (optimal) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 43.7 | 40.7 | 6.8% (0.0%) | 1.0 → 0.8 | 54% |
| 16 | 75.2 | 61.4 | 17.8% (15.0%) | 1.2 → 0.6 | 65% |
| 24 | 104.3 | 81.5 | 21.5% (22.8%) | 1.0 → 0.2 | 69% |
| 32 | 135.6 | 102.0 | 24.5% (24.8%) | 1.1 → 0.1 | 71% |

The pattern matches Prague and the density theory. Shareability scales with the cube of delay tolerance and saturates quickly ([Tachet et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5337932)). With few riders there are few combinations to choose between, so greedy is often already optimal: 16 of 30 eight-rider runs had a zero gap. The credible claim for judges is: **"On simulated peak waves of 16–32 students per gate, exact matching uses about 18–25% fewer car-km than first-come insertion; with fewer than 10 students the two are usually identical."** The claim to avoid is any version of the 65–71% savings against solo presented as the optimizer's win. Most of that saving comes from pooling at all, and the optimizer's contribution is only the gap to greedy. A savings figure near 70% also needs the stated explanation that destinations cluster on a few corridors. Four riders sharing one destination is the 75% ceiling.

## A demo that wins on honesty: baselines, metrics, stress tests

Judges are most likely to doubt over-claiming, so the simulation should be built to resist it. Use **three baselines on identical demand**, each seed fixed so every algorithm sees the same riders (common random numbers): everyone solo; greedy first-come insertion, which is `pooling/matcher.py` itself; and the bus reference of about 0.35–0.65 JOD ([Jordan News](https://www.jordannews.jo/Section-109/News/Normal-BRT-fares-go-into-effect-5735)). Only the greedy-to-optimal gap is evidence for the optimizer. Generate demand as a non-homogeneous Poisson spike after each class block, with destinations drawn from a clustered distribution, not a uniform one, since clustering drives match rate. Run 30 seeds and report means with 95% intervals. A one-car difference inside the interval is a tie and should be called one.

Report **seven metrics** with on-screen definitions: car-km saved versus solo; occupancy (passenger-km per vehicle-km); match rate (riders in cars of two or more, divided by all requests); students left alone, split into "no compatible partner" and "women-only, no match"; gate wait as mean and 95th percentile; per-rider saving as minimum and median; and cars dispatched. The real-world reference points are sobering. In Toronto, **only 52% of pooling requests were matched, and matched trips took 3.6 minutes longer** ([TRID](https://trid.trb.org/View/1759884)). Chicago matched about 72% of authorized requests in 2019 ([arXiv 2201.12696](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2201.12696)). Campus Connect can say it beats those rates in simulation only while showing the corridor clustering behind the result. For CO₂, state the factor (about 250 g/km, [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/greenvehicles/greenhouse-gas-emissions-typical-passenger-vehicle)) and the assumption that each pooled student would otherwise have ridden solo. Pooled ride-hailing roughly matches a private car's emissions once deadheading counts ([UCS](https://www.ucsusa.org/sites/default/files/2020-02/Ride-Hailing's-Climate-Risks.pdf)), and pooled services added **2.6 new vehicle miles per mile of personal driving removed** when riders came from transit ([Schaller](https://schallerconsult.com/rideservices/automobility.htm)). Saying this up front, rather than waiting for a judge to raise it, is itself a credibility signal.

The robustness panel should replay the same waves under realistic failure rates. Use no-shows and late cancellations at 3% (the paratransit figure, [TCRP](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/13856/chapter/6)) up to 10–15% as a stress test. Use 10–30% of riders declining their offered car, a women-only share swept from 0% to 50% of women, and a cold-start sweep of 4, 8, 16 and 32 riders per wave. The cold-start sweep matters most commercially: Waze Carpool's biggest stated challenge was density ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2019/10/11/catching-up-with-wazes-carpool-app)), and early UberPool matched only **7.9%** of requests ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2017/05/31/uber-uberpool-carpool/)). A slider-driven live run at 28+ riders per gate is also the right regression test for the timeout defect, which currently sends a full wave home alone.

## Recommended thresholds and objective

| Rule | Current | Recommended | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Detour | in-car km ≤ min(1.5 × solo, solo + 4 km) | Keep as a hard ceiling. Add a time form: extra in-car minutes ≤ min(50%, 10 min). Add side branches or OSRM distances so the cap can bind in the demo | Time drives acceptance; the cap never binds in the current corridors |
| Class-end gap (gate wait) | ≤ 20 min | Default ≤ 10 min. Opt-in "flexible, save more" up to 20 min, also offered to women-only riders | Commercial pools added 1–2 min; 10 min halves mean wait and still saves about 58% vs. solo |
| Shared window | ≥ 5 min | Keep | Not a binding issue |
| Saving floor | ≥ 20% per pooled rider | 25%, after the pruning fix. Quote riders their expected saving, not the floor | 20–25% is the market floor where pooled demand halved; actual optimal savings are 26%+ |
| Seats | 4 | Keep | Matches SP designs; the co-rider penalty is about 1 min |
| Women-only | hard filter | Keep. Show its cost. Optionally add DiDi-style soft priority, pairing women together when it costs nothing ([IFC](https://www.ifc.org/content/dam/ifc/doc/mgrt/executive-summary-ifc-gender-segregated-transportation-in-ride-hailing.pdf)) | Likely an adoption requirement in Jordan |
| Solver | car-km, then cars (two stages, 5 s each) | One weighted objective (below); prune only on hereditary rules; more workers | Removes the all-solo fallback and the missed-group defect |

Of the four candidate objectives, two turn out to be the same, and one is only half an objective. Because every car pays 468 fils plus 336 fils/km, total money saved equals the fixed sum of solo fares minus (468 × cars + 336 × car-km). **"Most money saved" is therefore car-km with each car counted as 1.39 km.** The current lexicographic rule is a special case, and switching to the money form costs no efficiency and fixes the timeout. "Most students sharing" counts the same thing as "fewest left alone", but without a tie-break it would accept any car-km. The recommendation is the fourth option: **fewest students left alone, then fewest money-weighted car-km.** Implement it as one CP-SAT objective, `Σ cars (336·km + 468) + P × (solo cars)`. In the 30-seed runs a very large P eliminated solo riders (0.8 → 0.3 at 8 riders, 0.6 → 0 at 16) for **4%, 2%, 0.5% and 0% more car-km** at 8, 16, 24 and 32 riders. Production systems likewise put a large penalty on unserved requests rather than only minimizing distance ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5255617)). The steelman for pure car-km is pathological cases. With four riders to a 16 km stop and one to 5 km in one wave, avoiding a solo rider forces a 3+2 split that costs 52% more car-km. So set P to a bounded value, for example 1.5 JOD (roughly the discount a solo student misses), rather than infinity. The pitch line then becomes: "we minimize what students pay in total, and we will spend up to 1.5 JOD of the group's savings to keep one student from riding alone."

## Conclusion

The research changes what Campus Connect should claim, more than what it should build. The defensible story is not "we use an optimizer, so we are optimal". It is that a campus gate after a class block is the one-origin, clustered-demand setting where pooling works, as Lyft's airport return suggests. In that setting exact matching beats greedy by a measurable 18–25% only when the wave is large, and the team can show where the gain comes from and where it disappears. Because the cost of the optimizer's rules is visible on a slider, the women-only filter, the wait window and the saving floor become product choices with prices attached, not hidden parameters.

The most urgent work is engineering rather than research. At realistic peak sizes, the current two-stage solve sends everyone home alone, and its pruning can drop the best car once savings rules start to bind. Both fixes take an afternoon. The objective change that fixes the first defect is the same one that makes the pitch clearer: one weighted cost, in fils, that judges can read as "money students keep, with a price on leaving anyone behind". The largest open uncertainty is the 20-minute gate wait. No published study measures tolerance for scheduled pooled pickups, so a 10-minute default with an opt-in longer window is the evidence-weighted choice until a pilot measures it directly.
